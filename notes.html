Integrus

An add-back is a story, and the buyer has heard all of them

The physician practice deal was my first time building an adjusted EBITDA bridge from scratch. QuickBooks exports in, normalized P&L out, then a list of adjustments: owner comp above market, a one-time legal bill, a family member on payroll. Each one nudged the number up and each one had to survive a buyer's associate asking "why should we believe that?"

What surprised me is how much of the valuation lived in that bridge and not in the multiple. Everybody roughly agrees on the comp range. The fight is about what the base is. So the most valuable skill in the room was not modeling, it was being honest about which adjustments were defensible before the other side made us.

Alden

Good business, sellable business. Not the same thing.

At the boutique I got to see a few companies that were clearly good, profitable, loved by their customers, and still hard to sell. Usually the reason was structural: revenue that walked out the door with the founder, one customer that was a third of sales, IP that was more "know-how" than anything you could put in a data room.

Mapping 30 to 40 buyers for one mandate taught me to think from the acquirer's chair. A strategic buyer is paying for something specific: a product gap, a customer list, a geography. If I can't name the thing in a sentence, the buyer list is a wish list.

V2WS

Selling 51% is a different pitch than selling 100%

Our pitch was Gap carving out Athleta and selling a controlling 51% stake instead of the whole thing. We got to a $561M enterprise value and a 19 to 20% buyer IRR, but the more interesting argument was structural: keeping 49% lets the seller share the turnaround upside while handing operational control to someone who will actually do the work.

The DCF and SOTP were the ticket in. The thing that made the memo good was explaining why each side would say yes. I want to get much faster at the first part so I can spend more time on the second.

DVC

How a small check gets to yes

I sat close to a $5.5M Series A extension and watched a convertible note turn into ownership on a cap table. The mechanics were fun to learn (caps, discounts, pro rata), but the bigger lesson was how much of an early-stage memo is really an argument about the founder. Market size and comps are table stakes. The vote turns on whether the partners believe this person will figure it out.

Also: automating the monthly KPI pull in Python felt small and turned out to be the most-thanked thing I did all internship. Nobody enjoys the pull.

EverGold

Most small businesses are not for sale

Screening 200+ lower-middle-market targets for a search fund taught me that "proprietary sourcing" is mostly patience. Owners are 60-something, they have never thought about EBITDA, and the ones who reply are often the ones with a reason to sell that they haven't said out loud yet. The screen was revenue durability, EBITDA quality, capex intensity, and owner-succession risk. Finding the ones that pass is slow.

That's where the AI workflows came from. Filings review, news scans, one-pagers. Fifteen hours a week back, which I spent on the calls and the memos, the parts that actually needed a person.

SEYA

The founder always leaves something out. I know because I did.

Running a brand and then diligencing other people's businesses in the same year was a strange, useful loop. Every time I read a target's numbers I could hear my seventeen-year-old self explaining away a returns rate or a "one-time" inventory writedown. It made me a more sympathetic reader and a much more skeptical one.
